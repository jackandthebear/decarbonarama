The average UK air source heat pump installation cost £13,943 in May 2026 according to MCS data. After the £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant that nets to roughly £6,400. What actually drives your quote is property size, cylinder and radiator work — not the brand on the box.

What the MCS data shows

The Microgeneration Certification Scheme tracks every certified heat pump installation in the UK, making its dashboard one of the most reliable sources of real-world pricing data. The numbers for 2026 are striking: the average air source heat pump installation in May 2026 cost £13,943, with the year-to-date 2026 average running at £13,645.

Average cost per kilowatt of installed capacity — up from approximately £1,000/kW in 2019. A 70% rise in seven years, significantly outpacing general construction inflation.

That trajectory deserves a closer look. Costs rose steadily through 2019–2021, spiked sharply in mid-2022 (coinciding with both the energy crisis and the Boiler Upgrade Scheme launch), dipped briefly, then resumed a climb through 2023–2025 before plateauing around current levels. The spike-and-plateau pattern is hard to ignore when you're asking whether the grant has shaped the very prices it was meant to offset.

Has the grant inflated prices?

The numbers invite an uncomfortable question. When the BUS launched in April 2022, the grant stood at £5,000. In October 2023, it was raised to £7,500. Both moments correspond to upward steps in the MCS cost data — though separating grant effects from genuine market inflation is difficult.

The mechanism is plausible. If a fixed government subsidy absorbs a large portion of the bill, competitive pressure on the total quote is dulled. Installers know the first £7,500 is underwritten — the price-sensitive conversation becomes one about the remainder, not the total. There's also the compliance overhead: MCS-registered installers face audit requirements, documentation, and ongoing certification costs that non-participating tradespeople don't carry. Those costs are real, and they appear in the quote.

"The grant has probably raised the floor. Complexity has raised the ceiling. Both things can be true simultaneously."

The counterargument is equally valid. Heat pumps are genuinely more complex to install than a boiler swap — they require detailed heat loss calculations, flow temperature optimisation, and often system upgrades including larger radiators or buffer tanks. The engineering skill required is substantively higher. And as volume has grown, more installations are happening in older, less well-insulated properties where the job is simply harder and more expensive.

The comparison that actually matters

The conventional framing — heat pump versus doing nothing — is the wrong one for most households facing a real decision. The right question is: what would you spend if your boiler needed replacing anyway?

A new gas boiler installed in 2026 runs between £1,800 and £4,800 all-in, with the Energy Saving Trust pricing an A-rated system with thermostatic radiator valves at around £3,700. A straightforward combi-to-combi replacement from a reputable installer typically lands at £2,500–£3,000.

That gap of roughly £3,000–£3,600 is what you're paying purely for the decarbonisation — the difference between swapping your boiler and going electric. One further factor narrows it modestly: heat pump installations currently attract 0% VAT until 31 March 2027, while a new gas boiler is subject to the standard 20% rate. On a £3,000 boiler job, that's around £500 of additional tax you'd pay that doesn't apply on the heat pump side.

💡 The Decarbonarma take

A decarbonisation premium of roughly £3,000–£3,500 — net of grant, net of the boiler you'd have replaced anyway — is the honest cost of switching. That's a meaningful number, but not an unreasonable one for a system that should last 20+ years and eliminates your gas bill dependency. The question isn't whether it's cheap. It's whether it's worth it for your home, your tariff, and your carbon goals.

What drives your specific quote

The MCS average of ~£13,600 spans very different properties and job types. The cost-per-kW metric (currently £1,720) gives a useful sanity check: if a heat loss calculation puts you at an 8kW requirement, expect a rough ballpark of £13,700–£14,000 — before adjusting for your specific circumstances.

The direction of travel

There are genuine reasons to expect costs to soften over the medium term, though not dramatically in the short run. The UK heat pump supply chain is maturing — Vaillant manufactures in Derbyshire, installer numbers are growing steadily, and the learning curve is flattening across the industry. Online-first providers are beginning to apply the same competitive pressure to heat pumps that platforms like Heatable and BOXT applied to boiler installation over the past decade.

The wildcard is the BUS grant itself. A higher grant would maintain or push up the current price floor. A reduction — which some installers and industry voices have called for on the grounds that it suppresses price competition — would create genuine market pressure but might also dent near-term demand. The government has extended the scheme to 2030 and committed further funding under the Warm Homes Plan, so the current structure looks stable for now.

Average ASHP install in 2026: £13,645. After the £7,500 BUS grant: ~£6,100.
